# Electromagnetic induction

## The effect

When a conductor cuts through magnetic field lines, an electromotive force (e.m.f.)
is induced across the ends of that conductor. If the conductor forms part of a
complete circuit, that e.m.f. drives an induced current.

Relative motion is what matters. Moving the magnet, moving the coil, or changing
the field strength all induce an e.m.f. A conductor held stationary in a steady
field induces nothing, because no field lines are being cut.

## Factors affecting the size of the induced e.m.f.

The magnitude of the induced e.m.f. increases with:

- the speed of the relative motion between conductor and field
- the strength of the magnetic field
- the number of turns on the coil
- the area of the coil enclosed by the field

## Direction of the induced current

Lenz's law states that the induced current always flows in the direction that
opposes the change producing it.

Pushing the north pole of a magnet into a coil induces a current that makes the
near face of the coil a north pole, repelling the magnet. Withdrawing the magnet
reverses the induced current, so the near face becomes a south pole and attracts
the magnet back. In both cases the induced effect resists the change.

This is a consequence of conservation of energy. If the induced current helped
the motion instead of opposing it, the system would accelerate itself and produce
energy from nothing.

## The a.c. generator

A rectangular coil rotates in a uniform magnetic field between the poles of a
magnet. Slip rings and carbon brushes connect the rotating coil to the external
circuit.

The induced e.m.f. varies as the coil rotates:

- **Maximum** when the coil is parallel to the field lines, because the sides are
  cutting field lines at the fastest rate
- **Zero** when the coil is perpendicular to the field lines, because the sides
  are moving along the field lines and cutting none

The output is a sinusoidal alternating e.m.f. Each complete rotation produces one
full cycle.

## Common examiner comments

- Candidates frequently write "the magnet creates a current". The current is
  induced by *relative motion*, not by the presence of the magnet.
- "Cuts field lines" earns credit; "moves near the magnet" does not.
- When asked for the direction of an induced current, state the opposing effect
  explicitly — naming Lenz's law without applying it rarely earns the mark.
- A question asking you to *explain* why the e.m.f. is zero at a particular
  instant requires the field-line reasoning, not just "the coil is vertical".

## Worked example

A coil of 200 turns and area $2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$ sits in a magnetic
field that falls uniformly from $0.40\ \text{T}$ to $0$ in $0.20\ \text{s}$.
Calculate the average induced e.m.f.

Rate of change of flux density:

$$\frac{\Delta B}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.40}{0.20} = 2.0\ \text{T s}^{-1}$$

Average induced e.m.f.:

$$V = N A \frac{\Delta B}{\Delta t} = 200 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.0 = 1.0\ \text{V}$$

The average induced e.m.f. is $1.0\ \text{V}$ (2 significant figures).
